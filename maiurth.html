AN INTRODUCTION

I. THE THESIS

In the spring of 1975, while every camera in America was pointed at the last helicopters lifting off the roof of the embassy in Saigon, nobody was watching a decommissioned missile silo forty miles outside Tonopah, Nevada. That was the point. Nine levels beneath the sagebrush, in a concrete shaft built to hold the end of the world, a man named Dr. Smoot was building a new one.

Four years earlier, at forty-one, he had published a thesis with a title so dry it nearly hid what it said: On the Compression of Living Matter and Its Transport to Designed Environments. In plain words, a way to make a person small, impossibly small, and send them somewhere that did not exist until someone drew it. His committee called it brilliant and useless. A man in Washington read it twice and called it a weapon. Two men in gray suits came for Dr. Smoot on a Tuesday, and he has not seen daylight since. He does not think of himself as a prisoner, or a patriot, or a traitor. He thinks of himself as a man solving a problem, and he has learned that the less he says about which side he is on, the longer he is allowed to keep solving it.

II. THE MEN WHO KEEP HIM

The program belongs to Giles Lane. He is enormous, a man who fills a doorway sideways, and when he is angry the rosacea climbs his cheeks like a brushfire. Lane believes in procedure the way other men believe in God. Every cruelty in the facility is typed, numbered, and signed in triplicate, and that, to Lane, is what makes it right. What the government wants from Dr. Smoot is simple enough to fit on one form: a prison no one can find, and a soldier no one can see.

Experiments need subjects, and subjects are where Augustin Angelos comes in. He is a big Black man in his fifties, always in a pressed charcoal suit, with a voice like the lowest string of a cello, a resonant baritone that makes every room he enters go quiet. Angelos serves no flag. He supplies Lane with "volunteers": drifters, deserters, hitchhikers, people the country has stopped counting. Lane despises him and cannot run the program without him. Angelos, for his part, finds Lane amusing and fully intends to outlast him. "Mr. Lane keeps the paperwork, Doctor," he told Smoot once, settling into a folding chair as if it were a throne. "I keep the people. Ask yourself which of us is truly in charge."

III. MY EARTH

At the bottom of the shaft is a pit, and suspended over the pit is a crystal the size of a man, glowing a cold, steady blue. Inside it turns a world no larger than a grapefruit: oceans, mountains, weather, a sky. Officially it is Test Environment Seven. Dr. Smoot, who has a scientist's weakness for bad puns, calls it MAIURTH. My Earth. He designed every coastline himself. Lane thinks it is a cage. Angelos thinks it is a market. Smoot has never told either of them what it really is, which is a door, and that one day he means to walk through it and not come back.

IV. THE HELLER BROTHERS

The order to get Dr. Smoot out came from no office anyone would admit to, and it went to the Heller brothers. Billy Heller, Army Special Forces, a Green Beret who did everything by the book and never let you forget that he had read it. Ben Heller, Navy SEAL, who had never met a rule he couldn't talk his way around, or a silence he couldn't fill with a wisecrack. On a moonless night in March they cut through two fences and got within forty feet of Dr. Smoot. Then the alarms went, and Ben took a rifle round through the thigh. As Billy dragged his brother back toward the wire, Smoot pressed something through the chain link: a scrap of paper, folded twice. It said, in careful block capitals, DON'T COME BACK FOR ME. COME BACK FOR THEM.

V. BETHESDA

Three weeks later, Ben is learning to walk again at the Naval Hospital in Bethesda, Maryland, and losing every argument to the nurse in charge of him. Rebecca LeBree is twenty-seven, the only daughter of a Connecticut shipping fortune, and her father has bought her everything she ever asked for except the one thing she wanted, which was to earn something on her own. She works the special operations ward by day and studies for medical school at night, and she has patched up enough commandos to be unimpressed by any of them. "You're healing nicely, Lieutenant," she tells Ben, "which is a shame, because you're much quieter on morphine." From the visitor's chair, Billy doesn't look up from his newspaper. "I've been telling him that since 1952, ma'am. You're the first one he's listened to."

The note sits folded on Ben's bedside table, between a paperback and a cup of cold coffee. Rebecca has read it, though she pretends she hasn't. Billy has read it a dozen times and wants to report it up the chain. Ben has already decided they are going back.

VI. THE PIT

In Nevada, nine levels down, Dr. Smoot stands at the rail and looks into the light. Behind him, a chair creaks, and that low cello voice fills the shaft. "It is a beautiful thing you have made, Doctor. A pity about the population." Smoot does not turn around. "There is no population." In the dark, Augustin Angelos smiles. "Not yet."

— END OF INTRODUCTION —
